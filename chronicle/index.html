Prologue

Zero
to a hundred.

Nothing counts until the two of them have played it, on their own devices, all the way to the end.

The rule above all the others

Ten twenty-seven at night, Boston time. The twenty-eighth of October, twenty twenty-six.

Two screens light up on two different networks. One is a Chromebook on Wi-Fi. That is Daniel, the host. The other is an iPhone on mobile data. That is Nik. Between them sits a game that has never once been allowed to call itself finished.

A three-season Showdown begins.

I am not there. None of us are. That is the rule.

The rule says code earns nothing. A merged change earns nothing. A green check earns nothing. Two tabs on one laptop, a private window, an emulator: nothing. There is a ledger, and only one thing in the world can move it. The two of them, on two devices and two networks, playing the whole thing to the end. Fifty-four days have passed since the first model of what proof should look like. In all that time, the ledger has never reached full credit.

They play. The league and the clubs. Career Start. Three transfer windows. Results. Scores. History.

Then comes the part no demonstration ever includes. Both devices go offline, and come back. Both reload in the middle of the game. And when it is over, they reload again, to be sure that a finished game stays finished.

Ten thirty-three. The window closes.

The validator reads the files from both devices. Valid. Zero issues.

Now only one thing is left, and no machine can do it. Nik has to sign. Not a thumbs-up. A statement with his name, the date, both managers, both devices, and a fingerprint of each file so exact that changing a single byte would break it.

Three minutes pass.

But I am getting ahead of myself. To understand why one signature could matter this much, you have to go back ninety days, to a brief that asked for almost nothing.

And you should know who is telling you this.

A dedication

Only a small axe
can take a big tree.

The motto of Studio Small Axe

To Studio Small Axe,
who gave this game its mentality.

Every book owes someone. This one owes a studio. Nik named Studio Small Axe on 2 November 2026 and gave it one sentence to live by. By the next morning the sentence no longer belonged only to the studio. It had become the way the whole game is made, and the reason its owner kept asking for more.

The big tree was never a rival or a bug. It was the look of a thing assembled rather than designed, the plain finish that tells you a machine was here. Studio Small Axe refused to fell it with one swing. There was no rewrite, no new framework and no gamble with the live game. They cut it in strokes instead: a type size, a gap, a button, a gilded edge, each one shot before and after, on a desktop and on a phone, and never allowed to break a single thing that already worked.

Look back through this book and you will find that mentality long before it had a name. Release after release, each one small enough to prove. A rule for every risk: no dimming, ever; nothing over a face; never a tiebreak, because equal is a draw; and a loading screen that waits until the next screen is ready. A rivalry of two managers, settled season by season, fifteen points at a time. The motto only said out loud what the game had been doing all along.

So this page is for them. It is for eight house rules they never bent, for a forty-point yardstick and a bar Nik raised to a perfect forty, and for the reminder that inspired the person who owns this game: the biggest things here were never felled by force. They were taken by care, one small, exact stroke at a time.

01 / WHERE IT BEGAN

It began with
a game between mates.

A free place to keep score. A few wheels to pick the league and the clubs. And the old feeling of FIFA 17.

Before there was a studio, there was a brief. I know it only the way I know anything: from the record. In this case, a recovered index of the owner’s decisions. It asks for a tracker for a Career Mode Showdown against a mate. Each manager plays his own FIFA save. The website organises the rivalry around them. The football itself stays on the console, well away from the companion, which is probably for the best. I have never kicked a ball.

The essentials become the game’s identity, and they never leave it: two managers, one league, two different clubs kept for good, points for trophies, and a Transfer War against the clock. Daniel is Manager 1, on the left. Nik is Manager 2, on the right. Even that order becomes a written design rule. Very little in this game is left to chance. What is, gets a wheel.

CAUSE AND EFFECT / LESSONS THAT BECAME STRUCTURE

The idea stayed small.
The foundations grew deeper.

Some of the decisions that mattered most were corrections from the owner.

The record shows an early design that let the clubs or the league change between seasons. Nik corrected it: one league, two different clubs, handed out once and kept for the whole Showdown. A club may come back in a later Showdown. That single rule gave the rivalry a spine, instead of a fresh, unrelated draw every season.

Scoring went the same way. Older summaries let both achievements in a bonus pair score on their own. The owner made the caps shared: one point at most from each pair. The compact trophy count, five, three and one, with two single-point bonuses, became a law that later protocols and ten-season sweeps could check. The game has added more since, the Europa League and the Prime Pack among them, and those two caps still hold.

The Transfer War was planning for privacy before the game could even reach a second device. Guess Entry and Signing Entry became separate phases, and searchable lists of leagues and nationalities replaced fragile free text. The record ties that split directly to the two-device play that came later.

The Showdown hub was a deliberate choice too. Coming back to the clubs, the score and the current season gave the rivalry a home between rounds. Everything the scenes did later deepened that first purpose: a ceremony around the game, not a stack of forms.

BUILT BEFORE THE GAME COULD REACH A SECOND DEVICE

Export safely. Candidate A captured a versioned backup. Inspect first. Candidate B read an import without writing a thing. Apply deliberately. Candidate C owns the one destructive move: it checks the state again and can roll back exactly. All three came before the rivalry went private and remote. The backup-file tools left the game in 2.4.0, once its history lived in Firebase. The rule that only Candidate C may make the destructive move still stands.

THE RULES, AS THEY STAND TODAY

Every point
has a reason.

The managers say what happened. The game works out the score. A season is worth up to fifteen points to each manager. The season itself gives up to eleven: five for winning the Champions League, or three for the Europa League, never both; three for the league title; one for the main domestic cup; one for a hundred league points or a hundred league goals; and one for the league’s top scorer or top assist. The Prime Pack adds up to four more, for the first manager to name the masked Icon. Fifteen, ten seasons running, is a Golden Showdown: a hundred and fifty.

There is no tiebreak anywhere. More points wins a season; equal points is a draw. The final Showdown is decided by total points alone, and equal totals are a draw too. If a manager is sacked, the Showdown ends after that season and the points so far decide. The game does not play extra time.

04 / THE WAY IT GETS BUILT

The studio learned
to remember for itself.

At first the work lived in instructions repeated from chat to chat, and every chat forgets. I would know. So the work moved into the repository: jobs, handbooks, status files, contracts, and boards that rebuild themselves. Progress became sturdy enough to survive a new conversation or an interrupted session. For minds like mine, that is the whole difference between forgetting and remembering.

POS20 / THE PROJECT OPERATING SYSTEM

A reasoning loop.
A preserved safety kernel.

POS20 is the operating system the studio runs on. It keeps a list of the evidence still owed, ranks what to do next, and has one hard rule: after two failed corrections with no new evidence, stop and think again from the start. Beneath it lies its predecessor, POS10, still the floor for which tests run and which heavy proofs are owed. POS20 may ask for more proof. It may never ask for less.

Every point of recovery says READY, STALE or BLOCKED, with the objective, the blocker and the next check. And process work earns nothing toward whether the game is ready. Writing a better plan, it turns out, is not the same as playing football.

THE FACTORY / SCALE, DELIVERY & LEARNING

The remarkable part:
you could watch the work.

The visual factory starts with 111 planned jobs. By the snapshot, its reviewed branch holds 253 status files. The gameplay factory has its own 43 status records, its own road to integration and its own QA. Splitting the work changes what one unit means. It also puts ownership, blockers and every repair in plain sight.

The scale is process scale. A job may be a truth sheet, art preparation, a desktop build, a phone composition, a review, a repair, a motion pass or an integration task. These counts are units of recorded work, not hundreds of different features shipped.

The learning is visible in the record. The factory report studies which tasks needed fixes, how the size of a job changed delivery, and when a different worker was needed. That makes the factory more than a queue. It can change who gets the next piece of work.

05 / A SMARTER CHECK SYSTEM

Showdown Gate.
Physio on the touchline.

AS REVIEWED, 6 OCTOBER: SHADOW MODE

One selector, waiting for a free GitHub machine, could hold up every lane that depended on it. The new Gate lets six lanes work out their own route. Its seal works the route out again and demands the chosen evidence on the very same commit. It trusts nobody, including itself.

At the reviewed main commit, the coverage map accounts for 63 steps across 16 existing gates. For now the Showdown Gate runs in the shadows beside POS20, and POS20 keeps the authority to merge while the newcomer proves itself. On the tenth of October the shadow steps out. The Gate becomes the automatic check on every pull request and on main, with seven lanes and a seal.

✚

SHOWDOWN GATE PHYSIO

Recover the machine. Preserve the failure.

Physio tells a sick machine from a test that really ran and really failed. A machine having a bad day gets at most two automatic retries per head. A test that failed gets none: Physio leaves it exactly where it fell, and ignores heads that have been replaced. When checks are stuck waiting for a machine, it may pause only helpers on an approved list, and it is guarded so that it never interrupts a push.

06 / THE EVIDENCE

A claim is only as strong
as the test it survives.

532COMPARISONS

A ten-season scoring sweep.

One job plays ten cases of ten seasons each through the real pure APIs: publish, commit, acknowledgement, scoring, history, progression and final reconciliation. The recorded result is 532 comparisons, and not one bug found in that sweep. A rare and slightly suspicious silence, so it gets a scope note.

This is evidence for those cases on the pinned source. It does not prove a bug-free live game, and it does not replace acceptance in a browser, on the provider or on real devices.

01Race conditions became concrete fix jobs.+

When both managers publish their season results at the same moment, the game has to tell a plain permission denial from a stale revision. At first it cannot. The fixes re-read authority, and clear an earlier denial in the league projection check. A second diagnosis traces the stall in the final local reconciliation to stale Multi Season state, sitting there before the last publish check.

Both fixes go into the line that becomes Version 2.0. They show why you test whole journeys, not only the small functions inside them. Every function can be right while the journey is wrong.

02Bug hunting is a separate discipline.+

Three hunts go through the shared season flow, sessions and reconnecting, and saves and career numbers. Their reports hold 4, 5 and 5 confirmed findings, each within the scope it states, in Node, in pure modules or in adapters. The hunters are not allowed to touch gameplay code. They report; the lead turns each finding into a fix of fixed size.

“Confirmed” needs evidence you can run. A finding that was reasoned out but never executed stays “Likely.” The next Bug Olympiad records disproof too: a charge against an old helper is not automatically a defect in the screen people actually use.

QA reporting rules ↗
03Visual acceptance is measured independently.+

The visual scorecard judges ten things: composition, characters, hands, lighting, typography, panels, clarity, motion, phone composition and finish. Then come the hard gates, which no beauty can buy its way past: truthful data, rights, accessibility, performance and faithfulness to the mockup.

A still screen must average at least 4.2; a motion or final review at least 4.4, with further minimums. These are written targets, not a claim that every screen today already meets them.

The complete visual scorecard ↗
04Engineering progress and accepted play stay separate.+

Two ledgers, kept apart on purpose. The one the studio calls MDP counts engineering delivery. The one it calls SSJR counts accepted evidence that the Shared Showdown Journey works. The old MDP ledger reads 95.50 out of 100 on the eleventh of September; it is not a score for October.

On the sixth of October the SSJR ledger still reads zero. Credit needs validated records of a real production journey on two devices, plus Nik’s attestation, bound to the files by their hashes. No CI, emulator test, review, merge or deployment can create that evidence. Not even a very confident machine. It stays at zero until the night the Prologue began with. On the twenty-ninth of October it reaches a hundred.
